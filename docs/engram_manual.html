ENGRAM FM

A unified quadrature modulation engine that works as a frequency shifter, ring modulator, and sideband processor. It adds custom carrier wavetable synthesis, phase modulation, recursive spectral feedback, and a full internal modulation system.

v1.0 Beta

How It Works

what it is, and the engine

Engram multiplies your incoming audio against an internal carrier oscillator through a quadrature (90°-phase) architecture, the same method used to generate single-sideband radio. That one path covers frequency shifting, ring modulation, and amplitude modulation, blending between them; a second algorithm swaps the shift for FM-style phase modulation. It follows the Bode frequency-shifter convention, so the carrier is the internal oscillator and the modulator is your input, the reverse of FM-synth naming.

Frequency shifting is not pitch shifting

Pitch shifting multiplies every partial by the same ratio, so the harmonic series is preserved. Frequency shifting adds a fixed Hz offset to every partial, which breaks it: shift a 440 Hz tone up 100 Hz and its 880 Hz overtone lands at 980, not 1080. The result is inharmonic, bell-like, metallic, and grows more extreme with the shift. Sub-Hz rates give slow, drifting tremolo; audio rates give ring mod and clangorous detuning.

The engine

The core runs at 4× oversampling; delay lengths, glide times, and the engine's filter cutoffs scale with the sample rate, so the sound holds at any host rate. (The envelope follower, LFOs, and output filter run at the base rate.) Your input is split into a 90°-phase pair by a polyphase IIR Hilbert filter (HIIR, Laurent de Soras), and the carrier supplies its own quadrature pair. The carrier is the multiplier at the heart of the path. How those two pairs combine is what the algorithm selects.

Additive: frequency shifting (SSB / ring mod)

The default. The carrier's quadrature pair multiplies the analytic input, and the two products sum to an upper and a lower sideband, the Bode-shifter and ring-mod path, translating every frequency by a fixed Hz amount.

Sideband math   USB = sigA·cosθ − sigB·sinθ    LSB = sigA·cosθ + sigB·sinθ. Sideband Blend equal-power crossfades between them: full lower shifts down, centre keeps both with the carrier suppressed (ring modulation), full upper shifts up.

Exponential: phase modulation (FM-style)

Here the carrier bends the input's phase instead of shifting its frequency. This one is not a multiply-and-sum: the filtered carrier value becomes an angle, and the analytic input is rotated by it, FM-style. Denser, brighter sideband clusters grow with depth.

PM math   the carrier is filtered first (so the filter shapes the modulation index while the rotator stays unit-magnitude), giving idx = PM Depth · m. The analytic input is then rotated by cos(idx) / sin(idx). That constant magnitude fixes the output to the upper sideband, so Sideband Blend does nothing here. PM Depth is the timbral control.

Character & Caveats

read this first

Engram is phase-dependent and inherently unstable. Frequency shifting continuously rotates the phase relationship between the channels and around the feedback loop, so the sound is never truly static. That is the point of the instrument. Engram is for auditioning and capturing more than for recalling an exact setting, and the same patch rarely sounds identical twice.

Small tweaks, large consequences   Feedback amount, filter resonance, carrier rate, and stereo spread all interact. A small change to any one can flip the system between cancellation (going quiet), resonant buildup, and runaway feedback. Move slowly, and keep Flush in reach to empty the feedback buffer.
Free-running phase   The carrier phase and the feedback loop keep advancing whenever the plugin processes (which most hosts do even while the transport is stopped), and nothing resets them on play, so the result depends on the exact moment audio arrives. With feedback up, tapping start/stop lands the loop in a different state each time; if a patch is not where you want, roll the transport and let it settle again. (Self-oscillation is a feedback effect, not the carrier's.)
Preset caveat   A preset captures settings, not sound. Because the effect is driven by your live input and is sensitive to carrier rate and feedback state, the same preset on different material can sound completely different. Presets are starting points, not repeatable results.

Approach & Starting Points

build up one thing at a time

Engram spans barely-there widening to dense, noise-like breakup, and the fastest way to get lost is to open every module at once. Build up in stages: carrier rate and sideband blend for the raw shift, then input and carrier filtering to choose what gets processed, then feedback for resonance and repeats, then modulation for motion. Because the effect is built from sidebands and sidebands multiply, keep one side (carrier or input) simple whenever you want the result to stay legible. When a patch collapses into noise, simplify the carrier or the input before touching anything else.

The factory library is the quickest way in. Each patch is built along these lines, so loading one and reading how it is put together, then taking it somewhere else, teaches the instrument faster than starting from silence. The techniques that shape a patch are documented in each module's own section: tuning the feedback tail and playing the loop with no input under Feedback, matching a crop to pitch under External Mode, and driving the sound from its own dynamics under Envelope Follower.

modulator pre-processing

The Input module conditions the incoming modulator signal before it enters the Hilbert transform. Filtering here decides which frequency content gets shifted. It is useful for isolating a band, or for cutting lows to avoid sub-octave blow-up at high shift amounts.

Carrier

the oscillator that shifts your sound

The carrier is the oscillator Engram multiplies your audio against; in effect it is the shifter. Its rate sets how far the spectrum moves, and its harmonic content sets the character, independently. The character comes from one mechanism: every partial in the carrier places its own shifted copy of your input around its frequency. A pure sine (one partial) gives a single clean shift; a richer shape stacks a sideband pair around each of its harmonics, which builds ring-mod texture and dense spectral clouds. The algorithm tabs decide whether the carrier shifts frequency (Additive) or modulates phase (Exponential).

A carrier is defined by its harmonic magnitude spectrum. Phase is largely inaudible in a steady tone (Ohm's acoustic law), and the carrier is a steady single-cycle read, so two different-looking cycles with the same harmonic magnitudes are effectively the same carrier, and the shape on the scope can mislead. Cropping a flute or a vowel does not print a flute or a vowel onto your sound; only its pattern of partials survives, as sideband placement. (Feedback is the one exception: recirculated phase re-enters through comb interference.)

Additive algorithm

The default, frequency-shifting mode. Every frequency in the input is translated by a fixed number of Hz, breaking harmonic relationships into the metallic, inharmonic, bell-like character of a Bode shifter or ring modulator. Two controls shape which part of the shifted spectrum you keep, and both belong to this algorithm. At the centre blend (the shipped default) both sidebands are kept with the carrier suppressed, which is ring modulation; pushing the blend toward either side gives a true single-sideband shift.

ControlRangeWhat it does
Sideband BlendLSB <> USBChooses which sideband survives. Fully lower shifts down, centre keeps both sidebands with the carrier suppressed (ring modulation), and fully upper shifts up. It is an equal-power crossfade between the three. In Exponential mode the rotator is a fixed single sideband, so this control does nothing.
Carrier Bleed0 → 100 %Leaks the carrier tone back into the signal as a DC offset before the Hilbert stage, morphing from suppressed-carrier ring mod toward full amplitude modulation. This DC-offset behaviour is additive-only; in Exponential mode the same dial works differently (see below).

Exponential algorithm

Phase-modulation mode. Instead of shifting frequencies, the carrier bends the input's phase, FM-style, generating clusters of sidebands around the input's own partials. It sounds closer to FM or phase-modulation synthesis than to a frequency shifter, ranging from subtle shimmer to harsh, harmonically dense clang. The timbral control here is PM Depth, below. Carrier Bleed also appears in this mode (in its own slot under Depth), but instead of the additive DC-offset trick it mixes the raw carrier wave straight into the output, so turning it up makes the carrier wavetable itself audible over the signal.

ControlRangeWhat it does
PM Depth0 → 10The modulation index, meaning how hard the carrier bends the input's phase. 0 is dry (no effect); low values add gentle sidebands and shimmer; high values pile on dense, bright, clangorous FM sidebands. This is the main tone control in Exponential mode.

Waveform Editor

drawing the carrier shape

You draw the carrier's waveform. The editor defines a single cycle from nodes and curved segments, and that shape becomes the carrier. A sine gives clean shifting; a shape full of harmonics scatters a sideband pair around every carrier partial (see Carrier), turning the same rate into ring-mod texture or dense clusters.

A harmonics overlay toggle in the utility bar shows the carrier's harmonics as bars along the bottom of the display, in both this editor and External Mode. The carrier is a single cycle, so the bars are its exact partials, log-scaled with the strongest at the top. They show the harmonic stack that drives the modulation. Display-only; the audio is unchanged.

On the overlay, the editor's built-in Sine is a smooth-curve approximation with a few faint upper partials. For a pure fundamental, load the factory Pure Sine shape from External Mode, a single-cycle sine baked bit-exact.

Feedback

recursive spectral delay

The feedback path taps the signal after the modulation engine and routes it back into the Hilbert input through a filter, a diffusion stage, and a delay line. Every pass is re-shifted, so feedback produces recursive spectral translation: repeats that decay, sustain, or build into self-oscillation depending on how much loop gain the patch supplies (see the crest note below). The returning signal is soft-clipped (tanh, ceiling near +24 dBFS) before re-entry, and the loop filters set which band recirculates; together they keep runaway bounded rather than blowing up digitally.

Amount & delay

ControlRangeWhat it does
Amount0 → 150 %How much of the output re-enters the loop. Low settings give repeats that clearly decay; as it climbs, the tail lengthens until loop gain reaches unity and the patch tips into sustain or self-oscillation. That tipping point is not fixed. It moves with carrier shape, delay, loop phase, and any resonant filter, so it can arrive well below 100% or never at all (see the crest note below). Raise it slowly and keep Flush in reach.
Delay1 ms → 16 sThe repeat interval, and, when self-oscillating, the pitch of the resonance (short is high and metallic, long gives rhythmic echoes).
Syncon / offLocks the delay time to host tempo bar divisions instead of the ms value.
Sync Division1/128 → 8 BarsThe bar fraction used when Sync is on. Default 1/4.
Sync ModeNOTE / DOT / TRIPStraight (Note), dotted (Dot, ×1.5), or triplet (Trip, ×2/3) feel on the delay's synced time. Labelled NDT on the panel.

Delay mode: PTCH vs FADE

This sets how the delay line reacts when its time changes, whether from a dial move, a sync switch, or modulation of the delay time.

PTCH (pitch) glides the read pointer smoothly to the new position, so a moving delay bends pitch exactly like a tape machine changing speed. This gives Doppler sweeps, and it is what lets a self-oscillating loop behave like a tuned, playable resonator. Use it for expressive pitch movement and for modulated delay sweeps.

FADE (crossfade) takes a different approach: when the delay jumps, the old and new tap positions are crossfaded over about 30 ms. There is no pitch glide; the delay relocates, changing the comb and modulation character cleanly. Use it for clean rhythmic delay changes and tempo-synced jumps where you don't want the pitch to slide.

Diffusion & stereo

ControlRangeWhat it does
Diffusion0 → 100 %Runs the feedback through a cascade of allpass stages that smear it across time. Each pass gets denser, so discrete echoes blur into a smooth, reverberant wash. This is the diffuser half of a reverb tank.
Stereo Spread±50 %Offsets the feedback amount between L and R, so the two channels decay at different rates for asymmetric, drifting tails.
Delay Spread±100 %Offsets the delay time between L and R (as a fraction of the base delay), giving ping-pong spread and Haas-style width.
Crossfeed0 → 50 %Feeds each channel's loop into the opposite channel before the delay, producing true ping-pong bouncing on the tail.

Filters

In-loop highpass and lowpass. These decide which frequency band recirculates, which is how you keep the loop under control: they prevent DC buildup and high-frequency runaway, and they set the tone of the tail.

ControlRangeWhat it does
HP / LP Freq2 Hz → 20 kHzHighpass (HP) and lowpass (LP) filter cutoff frequency in the loop. Raise the HP to roll lows out of the repeats; lower the LP for dark, tape-style tails.
HP / LP Q0.1 → 18.0Resonance (Q) in the loop. It compounds on every pass, so high Q can tune the whole tail to a pitch, or run away. Use sparingly.
Slope12 / 24 dBRolloff steepness: 12 or 24 dB/oct.
Stereo Spread±100 %Opposing L/R cutoff offset on the feedback filters.
Filter Poweron / offPowers the loop filter section. When off, the filters are bypassed, which is risky at high amounts since nothing is tempering the recirculating signal.
Feedback Poweron / offPowers the feedback path. When off, the loop stops being fed and the buffer drains naturally.
Carrier crest sets self-oscillation   Loop gain is proportional to the carrier's RMS level, and because every carrier table is peak-normalised, that works out to 1 / crest factor. A low-crest carrier (sine, square, saw) drives the loop hard and self-oscillates readily; a high-crest carrier (narrow pulse, noise, spiky wavetables) supplies less gain and may never run away. Crest is not the only factor: a resonant loop filter compounds the gain on each pass, and the phase relationship in the loop can add or cancel, so the same carrier can tip into oscillation from a much lower Amount, or refuse to. This is inherent to arbitrary carriers. The loop filters, delay, and Flush are the tools for managing it.
Play the loop on its own   With a low-crest carrier, set up a resonance, then mute the input (Input Bypass), and the self-oscillating loop keeps generating drones and evolving spectral tones with no source at all. Combined with delay-mode PTCH it becomes a tuned, playable resonator.

Output

mix & final stage

The final stage runs in this order: output gain drives the wet signal, then the output filter shapes it, then a clip/limiter stage catches the level, then the dry/wet blend mixes it against the dry input. Gain, filter, and clip all act on the processed (wet) path only; the dry path stays untouched until the blend.

The filter sits before the clip so the clip is the last shaping stage — a filter placed after the clip phase-shifts the clamped waveform and manufactures peaks above the ceiling (worst on bass), which is why the order was flipped. Output gain still sits before the clip, so it is a drive control rather than a level trim: backed off it stays clean, pushed it saturates into whichever clip mode is selected. Because the filter now feeds the clip, the GAIN meter and its live dBFS readout report the level hitting the clip (post-gain, post-filter). The post-clip output always pins at the ceiling and tells you nothing. The amber glow beside the mode button shows how hard the current mode is reacting.

ControlRangeWhat it does
Output Gain (Drive)−60 → +24 dBDrives the wet signal through the output filter and into the clip/limiter stage. It sits before the nonlinearity, so backing it off keeps the output clean and pushing it saturates. The coupled GAIN meter and dBFS readout show the resulting level hitting the clip (post-filter).

Reading the output stage   The fader shows the drive you dialled, the dBFS readout shows the level that drive produces at the clip, and the amber dot's brightness shows how far into the nonlinearity (or, for Limiter, how much gain reduction) the stage is working. All four modes share a 0 dBFS ceiling, so switching between them is a clean A/B of character alone. Both gain faders (input and output) also show a live level readout beside the value.

Modulation

sources, targets, and stereo motion

Seven sources drive targets across every module through the modulation matrix: the four LFOs, the Envelope Follower, and two MIDI-follow sources (note velocity and note pitch). The modulation is stereo to the core: the LFOs run as independent left/right pairs and most targets take a separate value per channel, so one LFO can pull the two sides of the carrier, the filters, or the feedback loop in opposite directions at once. Decorrelated motion like that is a fast way to open up width and depth.

Movement matters because the sidebands react to anything that moves. Sweeping a resonant filter slowly through the spectrum changes which sidebands survive from moment to moment, carving a moving formant out of the shifted signal. Slowly drifting the feedback delay time shifts the phase relationship around the loop, so a resonant feedback patch keeps evolving instead of sitting still. As a rule, slow and deep modulation of one well-chosen target does more than fast modulation of many.

The two MIDI sources come from the last note-on: velocity (soft to hard) and pitch (low to high notes), each as a value that rises from zero to full. They let a played note set filter cutoff, feedback, or carrier rate, so the effect responds to how and what you play. Both are mono. The sections below cover the Envelope Follower and the LFO bank in detail, then the matrix that routes every source to its targets.

Envelope Follower

modulation source, input dynamics

The Envelope Follower turns the input's loudness into a control signal for the modulation matrix, so the sound can drive its own processing. Louder passages can open a filter, push the shift, or deepen feedback, while quiet passages pull back. It is an attack/hold/release detector with a threshold gate, an output delay, and smoothing.

ControlRangeWhat it does
Depth0 → +36 dBSensitivity: gain in dB applied to the detected envelope. 0 dB is unity (the default); higher values lift quiet signals up so they still reach across the full modulation range.
Attack0 → 500 msHow fast the envelope rises to a peak. Short is snappy and follows transients; longer is smoothed and ignores fast spikes.
Hold0 → 500 msHow long it stays at the peak before releasing, holding the modulation up briefly after a hit.
Release0 → 16 sHow fast it falls after a peak. Short gives pumping; long gives slow, sustained sweeps that outlast the sound.
Threshold0 → 100 %A floor: input below it produces no output, and the region above is rescaled to full range. Ignores quiet material so only louder events modulate.
Offset0 → 500 msDelays the envelope output in time, so the modulation lands slightly after the sound that caused it.
Smooth0 → 1000 msLowpass on the output, rounding off sudden level changes for gentler, laggier motion.
ChannelSUM / L / R / MAXWhich input drives detection (mono mode): the sum, one channel, or whichever is loudest (MAX).
Stereoon / offIndependent L and R envelopes. NORM means each channel follows itself; MAX means both track the louder channel.

LFO Bank

four modulation sources

Four independent LFOs feed the modulation matrix. Each is a wavetable oscillator with a built-in random generator, so it can produce anything from a clean synced wave to organic drift to stepped chaos. Each LFO can run free or tempo-synced, be retriggered by MIDI, loop or fire once, and use a drawn shape or an external audio file as its waveform. A drawn shape loops as a single cycle; an external file is kept at its full length and swept across once per cycle, so a long or complex sample becomes a long, evolving modulation shape rather than a repeating waveform.

Editing the shape

Each LFO's waveform is drawn, exactly like the carrier's. An EDIT toggle on the LFO display reveals a node editor as an overlay on the live scroll, with a phase playhead running across it so you see where the LFO is reading. The gestures are the same as the carrier Waveform Editor (click to add a node, drag to move, right-click or Shift+click to delete, drag a diamond to bend a segment, double-click it to reset), and a SNAP toggle with its own grid divider sits beside EDIT while editing. This grid is independent of the carrier's. Edits are live: every gesture re-bakes the shape into the running LFO, and the drawn shape persists across session reloads until you load a fresh preset or an external sample. Editing is available in shape mode only; the toggle is hidden when the LFO is running from an external sample.

Modulation Matrix

routing sources to targets

The matrix routes the seven sources to targets across every module. Each cell sets a depth from one source to one target. Depths are scaled per-source (master scale) and then globally (global scale), so you can trim one source or the whole matrix at once. LFOs can modulate each other, so complex, evolving motion is possible from just a few sources.

Per-cell modes

Each routing cell carries two small toggles that change how the source is applied.

ToggleOptionsWhat it does
U / Bunipolar / bipolarLFO sources only. Bipolar (the default) lets the source swing the target both above and below its set value. Unipolar remaps the source to a 0-to-1 range, so it only pushes in the positive direction, more like an envelope.
M / Smono / stereoActive only when the source LFO is itself stereo. Stereo (the default) sends the source's left and right independently to the target's L and R channels; mono collapses the source so both channels get the same modulation. Most targets are stereo-capable. The genuinely mono targets (where a stereo source collapses to one value) are the envelope follower's parameters, feedback crossfeed, MIDI glide, and each LFO's own rate, glide, and spread controls. Carrier phase and the filter, rate, feedback, and delay stereo-spreads are per-channel instead: a stereo source drives their two sides independently.

Right-click a cell that carries a depth to disable it. The depth is kept but the cell contributes nothing to its target, and it renders dimmed. Right-click again to re-enable. This is a non-destructive mute: unlike Clear or a double-click reset, which zero the depth, disable holds the value so you can audition a routing out and back in without losing it.

Trigger and gate targets

Most targets take the source as a continuous value. A few are switches instead: the four LFO Retriggers, the global Retrigger All, Flush (a feedback-loop flush), and the six bypass targets (each filter bypass, the feedback module, and the input). These fire on an event rather than following the shape. A source fires one only when it climbs near the top of the cell's depth, so the depth doubles as trigger sensitivity: a full-depth square gives a clean gate, a thin slewed peak barely reaches, and below full depth several sources have to sum to cross the line. This is also why a Retrigger routed at a low depth no longer fires: it has to reach the top of the cell.

A bypass gate toggles its target relative to the manual button rather than overwriting it: with the button off the gate engages the bypass, with the button on it momentarily releases it, so a held button is never fought and its stored state is untouched. The crossfade is click-free. Flush empties the feedback loop on each rising edge, the same flush the manual button performs, so a source can retrigger it rhythmically.

Routing dots   The moment you enter a depth in a matrix cell, a small purple dot appears on that target's label out in the panels (beside the caption of its dial, strip, or filter handle) and on the LFO tabs. Preset System

saving and loading

Presets live in one shared library folder on disk, used by every instance of the plugin. A preset is one of two kinds. A full patch stores the whole plugin state. A carrier wavetable preset stores only the carrier shape, so it grafts onto whatever patch is live rather than replacing it. Either kind is self-contained: it embeds the carrier audio and any external LFO audio it uses, so a preset carries everything it needs and can be moved between machines.

Saving

SAVE sits on the main preset bar in the title area and opens the save dialog, where you set a name, a folder, optional labels, and a note. Two toggles sit alongside. CARRIER WAVETABLE ONLY saves just the carrier shape instead of the full patch. CROP ONLY, available when the carrier is in external mode, embeds only the cropped region of the loaded audio rather than the whole source file; the preset is smaller, though its crop is then fixed and cannot be re-adjusted after loading. A full save captures:

CapturedDetail
ParametersThe value of every parameter in the plugin.
Carrier waveformThe node curve in shape mode, or the embedded carrier audio in external mode (the whole file, or just the crop when CROP ONLY is set).
Modulation matrixAll active routing cells.
LFO shapesEach LFO's shape by name, plus the actual audio embedded when that LFO is in external mode.

Loading a full patch overwrites

Loading a full patch returns the plugin to exactly the state that was saved. Every parameter is first reset to its factory default and the modulation matrix is cleared, then the preset is applied on top, so anything the preset does not set lands on its default and stray routing from the previous patch is gone. You do not need to INIT first. A carrier-wavetable preset does the opposite: it grafts its shape onto the live patch, swapping the carrier waveform and leaving every other setting untouched, which is how you audition carriers under a patch you are building. (Factory reset here means the shipped defaults, not any you have set with Set as default; INIT is the path that honours your own defaults.)

LFO presets work differently

Each LFO has its own preset bar that browses the same shared library, with its own SAVE button. Saving stores the current drawn shape together with that LFO's own control settings (rate, sync, stereo, random, jitter, glide, smooth, offset, phase start, and one-shot) as an instance-neutral shape preset (shape mode only; external samples are not saved this way). Loading a shape onto an LFO reads the waveform and, when the preset carries them, restores those stored control settings. A shape that carries no stored settings loads the waveform alone and leaves the LFO's own parameters where they were. INIT on an LFO resets that LFO's own parameters and reloads the default sine. You can build the library from either the main SAVE dialog or an LFO's SAVE button, and any LFO can pull a shape back out of it.

Single cycle vs full sample   Drawn shapes, and any external audio loaded onto the carrier, become a fixed 2048-sample single cycle: one period read at the oscillator rate. An LFO handles external audio differently. It keeps the whole cropped region at its native length and sweeps across it once per cycle, so a long, multi-cycle crop plays back as a long, evolving contour rather than a repeating single cycle. That makes complex samples, and presets built from them, well suited to LFO shapes: a single preset can drive a slow, non-repeating modulation that never quite lands in the same place twice.
Presets store settings, not sound   A recalled preset can sound very different on new source material or at a different carrier rate. See Character & Caveats for why.
